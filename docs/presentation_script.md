## 0:45–2:05 — Ziying Zhou — Slide 3

“We started with a campus map showing the nearest restroom and directions. We then explored cleanliness and accessibility through interviews. The more specific problem was discovering important details only after arriving.

“One Columbia student, A04, found an open café nearby on Google Maps. She told us, ‘One time the bathroom was downstairs and the stairs were really narrow.’ She was carrying a large bag. She still used the restroom, but only learned about the difficult route after getting there. She wanted to know the floor, stairs and elevator information beforehand.

“A Midtown resident, C05, described a different consequence. While playing tennis, she found dirty portable toilets with no handwashing option. She waited and ended tennis early to find a restroom elsewhere. We didn't record the amount of time lost.

“These accounts helped us focus on NYC residents and visitors in unfamiliar neighborhoods. Today's demonstration follows one decision: finding an option without stairs before committing to the walk.”

## 2:05–3:10 — Zhenni Yue — Slide 4

“We organized the app around three questions.

“First, entry. Does the place require a purchase or a code? Second, the route inside. Which floor is the restroom on, and are there stairs or an elevator? Third, conditions and evidence. What did someone report, and when?

“A04 helped us see why a general accessibility label needs supporting route details. C05 showed why the existence of a restroom doesn't tell us whether someone would choose to use it. So we keep entry, route information and cleanliness separate.

“Our MVP lets someone inspect those details beside a location and then open directions. Reports keep their sources, and unknown information stays unknown. The goal is to give someone enough information to make a choice before walking over.

“Zheli will show that through one example, starting on the map.”

### 3:10–3:50 — Set up the situation and filter

“Imagine Joslyn on Fifth Avenue looking for a restroom. She uses a wheelchair, so she wants to understand the route from the street before choosing a place. This is an example scenario based on the information gap we found in our interviews.

“She opens the map and selects Accessible and Stair Free. She can combine these options. Accessible shows a reported accessibility status, while Stair Free looks for explicit information about the route.”

[Open Filters. Tap Clear filters, select Accessible and Stair Free, then Done. Keep Clean, Public and Elevator access unselected.]

### 3:50–5:05 — Choose the pin and inspect the route

“Here she finds The Peninsula nearby. The card gives her the address, and she can open the details before heading over.

“This contributor report says the restroom is available 24/7. It describes step-free doors on the right side of the main entrance. Inside, she can take the elevator, choose Penthouse and reach the 23rd-floor restroom, with zero stairs after entry.

“That answers a more useful question than distance alone: which entrance should she look for, and how would she reach the restroom inside?

“We also keep the limits visible. These are reported details. The actual visit date wasn't provided, and the report doesn't confirm current elevator operation, entry permission or stall suitability.”

## Q&A reference — Slide 7

**What changed because of the research?**

“Our starting idea centered on the nearest restroom. A04's experience directed us toward the route inside, and C05's experience showed why conditions can change someone's choice. We now separate entry, route and dated observations.”

**Does this certify wheelchair accessibility?**

“No. Accessible is a reported status, and the route details provide additional evidence. Users still need to assess the particular entrance, route and facilities. A missing observation date or unknown feature stays visible.”

**Did a wheelchair user from the interviews complete this task?**

“Joslyn is our illustrative demo scenario. A04 described narrow stairs while carrying a large bag and still used the restroom. The record doesn't say she used a wheelchair. We still need to observe this workflow with people who rely on the relevant access features.”

**What is live and what is hypothetical?**

“The app, map, filters, saved places and reported venue details are implemented. Joslyn's visit and five-star response are the scenario used to explain the workflow. We don't publish a rating for that fictional visit.”

**How much demand have you proved?**

“We need to separate outreach activity, signup records and actual product use. We would report only verified results for each, rather than treat campaign goals or interview interest as adoption.”
